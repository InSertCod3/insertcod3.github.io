Plot twist: after a lot of time staring at screens, I picked up a welding gun. Specifically, a MIG welder. And I haven't touched one in about ten years.

Turns out welding is a lot like riding a bike, if the bike was on fire, occasionally spat molten metal at you, and you'd forgotten how to ride it.

## The rust was not just on the metal

My first few beads looked like a pigeon had a bad day on a steel plate. Lumpy, uneven, and generously decorated with spatter. Ten years away will do that. The muscle memory was in there somewhere, just buried under a decade of keyboards and good intentions.

## Things I relearned (the hard way)

- **The sizzle is feedback.** A good MIG weld sounds like bacon frying. A bad one sounds like bacon arguing with you.
- **Settings matter.** Wire speed and voltage are a dance. Get them wrong and you'll find out immediately, loudly.
- **Prep is everything.** Clean metal welds better. Dirty metal welds like it's holding a grudge.
- **Your hands need to be steady.** Mine, apparently, had been replaced with a pair of nervous jellyfish.
- **Wear the gear.** Helmet, gloves, long sleeves. Looking cool is optional, not getting burned is not.

## Why it's weirdly like programming

Here's the thing I did not expect: welding and debugging have a lot in common. You change one variable, see what happens, adjust, and try again. The feedback loop is fast and brutally honest. A bad weld doesn't care about your intentions, and neither does a failing test.

The difference is that when a weld goes right, you can pick it up, hit it with a hammer, and watch it stay put. Try that with a merged pull request.

## The verdict

It's been fun, challenging, and properly rewarding. Every bead that comes out a little straighter than the last one feels like a tiny win. I'm still very much a beginner, so I'm not about to build anything structural, and nobody should park a car on my work just yet.

But I'm hooked, and I'll probably write more about it as I get better (or at least as I get less spattery).
